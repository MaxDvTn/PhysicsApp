Solar day vs sidereal day: why does the day last 23 hours and 56 minutes?

A guide to spherical astronomy and the Earth's motions for Year 3

1. Introduction: two different ways to measure a day

From childhood we learn a simple and apparently unbreakable rule: a day lasts exactly 24 hours. It is the basic rhythm of our clocks, our daily routines and the organisation of the whole of society.

Yet if we look at the Earth from a purely physical and kinematic point of view, we run into a fascinating paradox: to make a complete 360° turn about its axis, our planet takes only 23 hours, 56 minutes and 4 seconds.

Where do these 4 minutes or so of difference come from? Why is daily life based on a 24-hour period if the Earth's physical rotation is completed earlier? The answer lies in the difference between two very different reference points: the Sun and the distant stars.

2. The basic definitions

To understand the time difference, we first need to be clear about which reference frame we use to measure the start and the end of a daily cycle.

Type of dayReference pointAverage lengthPhysical definition
Sidereal dayFixed stars (celestial background)23 h 56 min 04 sTime the Earth takes to make a 360° rotation about its axis.
Solar daySun24 h 00 min 00 sTime between two consecutive culminations of the Sun on the same meridian.
  • Sidereal day: the time needed for the Earth to make a full 360° rotation with respect to the “fixed stars”, thought of as the basic inertial reference frame of the celestial sphere. If we watch a distant star pass exactly over a given meridian, the sidereal day ends when the same star is back in the same position.
  • (Mean) solar day: the time between two consecutive passages of the Sun across the local meridian (that is, astronomical noon, or culmination). By civil convention, this interval has been fixed at exactly 24 hours (86,400 seconds).

3. The geometric mechanism (why do they differ?)

The basic cause of the difference is that the Earth does not only spin on itself: at the same time it moves along its orbit around the Sun, in a motion of revolution.

Kinematic quantityComputed value
Complete turn of the Earth's orbit360°
Length of the solar year~365.25 days
Daily orbital advance360° / 365.25 ≈ 0.986° per day
Rotation speed of the Earth360° / 24 h = 15° per hour (1° every 4 min)

While the Earth spins on its axis (anticlockwise, seen from the North Pole), it also moves along its orbit, advancing by about 1° per day (0.986° to be exact).

So, after completing a 360° rotation with respect to the fixed stars (the end of the sidereal day), the Sun is no longer in the same place in the observer's sky. Because the Earth has moved along its orbit, the Sun appears to lag behind.

For the Sun to be exactly back on the observer's meridian (completing the solar day), the Earth has to make an extra rotation equal to that angle of orbital motion (about 1°).

Since the Earth rotates with an angular speed of

360° / 24 hours = 15° per hour ⟹ 1° every 4 minutes

it follows that the extra degree of rotation needed to line the Sun up again corresponds to about 4 minutes of extra time.

4. How did the ancients measure it without atomic clocks? (Kuhn's view)

In the first chapter of The Copernican Revolution, the historian of science Thomas Kuhn reflects on how ancient astronomers (Babylonian, Greek, Alexandrian) managed to grasp these celestial motions with extraordinary precision long before telescopes and precision clocks were invented.

The ancients did not measure time with the caesium atom, but they observed events with great systematic rigour:

  1. Stars rising earlier: astronomers noticed that a given star did not rise at the same solar time every evening, but always came up about 4 minutes earlier each night.
  2. The yearly build-up: over a whole solar year (about 365 days), this daily gap of 4 minutes keeps adding up. Multiplying 4 minutes by 365 days gives about 1,460 minutes, that is, about 24 hours. In other words, in one year the Earth makes exactly one more turn with respect to the stars than with respect to the Sun: 365 solar days correspond to about 366 sidereal days.
  3. The calculation: dividing the length of a day (24 hours = 1,440 minutes) by the number of days in the year (about 365) gives the daily shift:

1,440 minutes / 365.25 ≈ 3 minutes and 56 seconds

By measuring the position of the zodiac constellations at dawn and at sunset as the seasons changed, ancient cosmology was able to quantify the apparent motion of the Sun along the ecliptic with great accuracy.

5. Is it the same everywhere?

Yes. Whether the observer is in Italy, in the Netherlands or right on the equator, the difference between the solar day and the sidereal day is the same.

It is in fact a global kinematic property linked to the combination of the Earth's two motions (rotation about its axis and revolution around the Sun). It does not depend on the observer's geographical coordinates: latitude and longitude change the apparent height of the stars or the tilt of the horizon, but not the angular speed at which the planet moves along its orbit and spins on itself.
